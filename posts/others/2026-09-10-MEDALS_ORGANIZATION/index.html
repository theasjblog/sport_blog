What Do You Do With All Those Race Medals?

Race often enough and the medals, T-shirts, swim caps and bibs start piling up. Here is how I keep the memories without letting race memorabilia take over the house.

If you race often enough, eventually you develop a problem nobody really warns you about.

Stuff. Lots and lots of stuff.

T-shirts. Swim caps. Race bibs. Finisher medals. Podium medals. The occasional plaque or trophy. And probably a few other random things that seemed perfectly reasonable when someone handed them to you at a finish line.

I have drawers full of race T-shirts, a growing collection of swim caps, and well over 100 medals.

So, what do you actually do with all of it?

Over the years, I have developed a bit of a system. Nothing particularly sophisticated, but it keeps most of the race memorabilia organized without turning the house into a shrine to local 5K races.

The T-shirt problem

Let’s start with T-shirts.

I have too many of them. I really, really do not need any more.

This is why I am quite happy that race organizers such as MultiSport Canada let you choose whether you actually want a race shirt. Some people love collecting them, and that is great. They can have theirs.

As for me? Unless there is something particularly interesting about the shirt, I am perfectly happy to say no.

I still use the ones I already have. Some are in my normal training rotation, and eventually they will wear out and make room for others.

I have also seen people do much more creative things with old race shirts: quilts, pillows, wall art and all sorts of other projects. Maybe one day I will do something similar.

For now, though, my strategy is much simpler:

Wear them until they die.

And then there are the swim caps

Race swim caps are a slightly different story.

They are generally not the greatest caps in the world, and I would not normally choose one over my regular swimming caps. But they are useful.

I always throw a couple into my swim bag as backups. Chlorine eventually destroys everything, so having a spare cap around is handy if my good one breaks. They are also useful when somebody at the pool needs to borrow one.

The only problem is that I seem to collect them much faster than I can destroy them.

Still, compared with medals and T-shirts, this is a relatively minor storage problem. A surprising number of swim caps fit into a small box, so for now I am just letting the collection grow.

Ask me again in ten years.

But what about the medals? And the race bibs?

This is where things get more interesting.

First, I think we need to distinguish between two very different kinds of medals:

finisher medals and podium medals.

I like both, but I definitely do not treat them the same way.

Finisher medals: my race photo albums

I like race bling.

I know a finisher medal is not necessarily something particularly exclusive. It is the medal everybody gets for crossing the finish line. And since I am not exactly doing 100-mile ultramarathons, some of my medals come from races that almost anyone with a reasonable amount of preparation could finish.

A 5K finisher medal is not exactly an Olympic gold medal.

But that is not really the point.

When I look at an old medal, I remember the race.

I remember travelling there. I remember who came with me. I remember the weather. I remember whether I had a good race or a terrible one. Sometimes the race was part of a little family holiday. Sometimes something funny happened before or after it.

The medal itself may not be particularly valuable, but the memories attached to it are.

So I want to keep them.

The problem is how.

Throwing 100 medals into a drawer or a box technically solves the storage problem, but then I am never going to look at them again. Hanging them all on medal racks works—and there are plenty of nice commercially available ones—but once you have a lot of medals, that can become a bit much.

I wanted something compact that would still let me actually look through them.

So I turned my medals into books.

My DIY medal book

The basic idea is very simple.

I use six-pocket plastic binder sheets, the type normally sold for coin collecting. I then buy some thick black paper and cut it to fit inside the individual pockets. This is optional, but it gives each pocket a nice background and helps visually separate the two sides of the plastic sheet.

Each race gets its own little layout.

On one side, I put the medal itself into one of the pockets.

In another pocket, I add some basic information about the race—date, distance, finish time, position or whatever else seems worth remembering.

Then I use the remaining pockets for a few small photos from the day.

It basically becomes a miniature scrapbook page for the race.

On the opposite side, I normally put the race bib together with a small section cut from the medal ribbon.

I like keeping part of the ribbon because it often has the race name, year or sponsor printed on it, and it is part of the design of the medal. But keeping the entire ribbon would take up far too much space.

So: scissors.

Sorry, medal collectors.

Everything then goes into a normal binder.

And this is the part I really like about the system: it is incredibly space efficient.

I currently have more than 100 medals stored in three binders. All three comfortably fit inside a single square of an IKEA Kallax, with enough room left for another couple of binders.

That means potentially hundreds of race medals can occupy roughly the same amount of space as a small stack of books.

No giant medal rack. No wall covered in ribbons. No box full of tangled medals.

But they are also not hidden away.

Whenever I feel like it, I can pull out a binder, flip through it and take a quick trip down memory lane.

What you need to make one

The other advantage is that this is a pretty inexpensive solution. If you want to try it yourself, there really isn’t much to it.

My setup uses:

  • A standard ring binder — nothing special required. I would just recommend something sturdy, as medals are heavy and put strain on the binder.
  • Six-pocket coin-collector sheets — these are the key part of the system and are just about the right size for most medals.
  • Black cardstock — cut to size and used as a background inside each pocket.
  • Printed photos — I usually pick a few that remind me of the race.
  • Scissors or a paper cutter — for the cardstock, race bibs and the sacrilegious cutting of medal ribbons.

None of this needs to be expensive. I would just buy whatever versions are readily available rather than worrying about particular brands.

Making it personal

Once you have the supplies, all you need is a little time to put the pages together. And because you make every page yourself, you can include whatever information matters to you.

Maybe that is your finish time and overall position. Maybe it is your splits. Maybe it is a photo with your family. Maybe it is the elevation profile of a particularly horrible course.

There are no real rules.

That is probably why I prefer this to simply hanging all the medals on a rack. The medal becomes only one part of the memory rather than the whole thing.

Podium medals are different

Podium medals get special treatment.

First of all, I do not have nearly as many of them.

If your podium-medal-to-finisher-medal ratio is significantly better than mine, good for you, but you might have to find a different solution.

More importantly, though, they mean something slightly different to me.

A finisher medal reminds me of an event and an experience. A podium medal also reminds me of the work and planning that went into being competitive on that particular day.

That does not mean somebody who did not make the podium worked any less hard. It is simply how I look at my own medals.

They are harder for me to get, so I want to display them differently.

For those, I use small frames with space for one medal and one photograph.

The medal goes on one side and a podium photo goes next to it.

Each frame is roughly the size of an A4 sheet of paper. I currently have around ten of them hanging in my home office.

So yes, unlike the medal binders, these do consume some wall space.

But ten relatively small frames are still manageable, and I like having those particular memories visible rather than tucked away on a shelf. I like to hang them up using removable strips, as that allows me to rearrange them if I want to.

And the random stuff…

Then there is the category I can only describe as random race stuff.

Occasionally you get something that is not a medal at all.

I have a couple of plaques from MultiSport Canada for their season series, along with the occasional trophy and other bits and pieces accumulated over the years.

Those are much rarer, so I do not really have a storage system for them.

They just get displayed.

Fortunately, I have a huge 5×4 Kallax behind me in my home office, which provides plenty of random little spaces for plaques and trophies to live.

At least until I fill that too.

In the end, it is about the memories

None of this memorabilia has much intrinsic value.

A race T-shirt is a T-shirt. A swim cap is a piece of silicone. A finisher medal was probably handed to hundreds—or thousands—of other people on exactly the same day.

But I do not keep these things because they are rare.

I keep them because they are little physical reminders of things I did.

Some remind me of good performances. Others remind me of terrible races that are funny in retrospect. Some bring back memories of travelling somewhere new, racing with friends, having my family waiting at the finish line, or simply spending a Sunday morning doing something I enjoy.

And that is why I particularly like the medal books.

They do not really display the medals.

They store the stories attached to them.

And somehow, more than 100 of those stories still fit comfortably into one square of an IKEA Kallax.
