Wasaga Beach Sprint Duathlon

A season-closing duathlon that turned into a small tactical battle and a bigger lesson in paying attention to the course.
race_reports
duathlon
Author

Adrian Joseph

Published

August 29, 2026

Pre-race

This race had a slightly odd place in my season.

On paper, it was not the biggest target. It was not a world qualifier, and by late August I was already at the point where the main work of the season was done. But it still mattered to me for two reasons.

First, I wanted to finish the year properly. Second, there were still useful points available for the MultiSport Canada duathlon series. So even if this was more of a B race than an A race, I still wanted to race it well.

I had also been here before, two years earlier, for the sprint triathlon. I did not enjoy that version of the event at all. The swim had one of those awkward long entries and exits through shallow water where it is too deep to run well and too shallow to swim properly.

Still, a duathlon is a different story. No swim, no awkward water entry, and a course format that at least gave me a reason to give the place another chance.

There was also a bit of self-imposed pressure in the background. Two years ago I finished 5th overall here, albeit in a different event. And coming into this race I had already raced three times this season and finished 2nd, 2nd, and 3rd. So the question in my head was fairly simple: could I make it four podiums from four starts?

A Better Build-up

Compared with the build-up to Barrie, this one was much more normal.

That may sound like a low bar, but after the ER visit and the blood pressure medication drama earlier in the month, “normal” was a meaningful upgrade. Training was not perfect, but it was uninterrupted, and I came into the race feeling more like an athlete preparing for a race and less like someone trying to improvise their way through damage control.

I still would not say I felt especially sharp. It was late in the season, and there is a difference between being healthy enough to race and being truly primed to race. But I was in a much better place than I had been three weeks earlier.

That changed the goal a bit. In Barrie, the first objective was just to race safely and get around. Here, the objective was to race properly.

Race morning

The warm-up was fine, but not especially inspiring.

I felt a bit sluggish, and the main thing I noticed was some tightness in the back of the legs. Not full cramping, not quite panic-inducing, but enough to put the thought in my head that this might become relevant later.

On the start line I noticed a few guys who looked strong, plus Michael Shay, who had finished behind me in Barrie and ended up 3rd in our category there. He had been almost six minutes behind me that day, but that does not mean much: you never know if somebody else is also going through a rough patch in their preparation.

There were also a bunch of under-19 athletes at the front who started with the kind of enthusiasm that usually means one of two things: either they are very good, or they are about to have a difficult second half of the race.

Run 1

I started, as usual, with the intention of being sensible.

Also as usual, that intention lasted about five seconds.

The plan had been to keep things around 4:20/km early on and then reassess. Instead, the first look at the watch showed something like 3:45/km. After that it settled more into the 4:05 to 4:10/km range, but by then the damage was already being negotiated rather than avoided.

Michael and I ran together for roughly the first half of the opening 5K. Then he started to drift away. I could tell that matching him would cost me too much, so I let him go and focused on running my own race, confident that I had good biking legs.

The closing part of the run was harder. The pace started to slip, more toward the 4:25 to 4:35/km range, and I was already asking myself the familiar question of whether I will ever learn how not to overcook the first run of a duathlon.

Even so, the damage was manageable. The final results show that I had the 3rd fastest run in my category and 9th overall, which kept me very much in the race. But I did not know that at the time, in part because it was a wave start.

This time there was no clear drop, except for run cadence after 15 min. Rather, there was a constant decline. This was caused by a lack of race fitness, but also by me trying to control the pace. The drop in cadence is a bit concerning; I’ll need to work on that.

T1

This time, at least, transition did not turn into a comedy routine.

Two years prior in Wasaga I lost over a minute hunting for my bib number like an idiot. Here, T1 was much cleaner. I still had the usual small struggle getting my feet into the shoes, but only the left one caused any real trouble, so by my standards that counts as efficient.

The numbers back that up. I had the fastest T1 in my category and the 2nd fastest overall, which moved me from 3rd to 2nd in the Men40-49 race. Again, I did not know this at the time, and this will come into play later.

Bike

Once onto the bike, the race settled down.

This is normally the leg where I feel most in control, and that was true again here. The goal was straightforward: ride strongly, reel in anyone available to reel in, and build enough of a gap that the final 2.5K would become a matter of managing the advantage rather than trying to create it from scratch.

What made the bike leg slightly confusing was that I did not have a great read on where my direct competition actually was.

By the turnaround, I still had not seen another obvious rider from my category ahead of me. Before I reached the turnaround, I saw one of the duathletes in my category, too far ahead for me to catch, and a few of the younger guys who had gone out hard on the first run, but I had not seen Michael Shay, which was concerning. With only about 10K left at that point, every minute matters, and I did not feel as though I had created much separation.

Then, shortly after the turnaround, I saw him behind me.

That was a surprise, because I am fairly sure I had not passed him on the road. The most likely explanation is that I got him in transition, which would also fit with what happened in Barrie, and the Sportstats numbers confirm this. Either way, it clarified the race. I knew I had someone I needed to stay ahead of, and I knew the margin was not large enough to relax.

So I pressed a bit more.

I wanted at least 20 seconds, ideally more. In the end I came off the bike 2nd in category and 3rd overall, with a 1:38 advantage over Michael Shay and still with a real shot at the podium. I will repeat myself, but I did not know any of this. In my head Michael was just a few seconds behind me.

The data is telling us that it was a very even ride. Not too hard, as the HR went down from the run, and pretty consistent. If only I could run in the same way…

T2

T2 was uneventful, which is exactly what you want.

I got through cleanly, albeit a bit slowly, and started the final run still 2nd in category. At that point the race had become very simple: hold the place if possible, and at the very least make anyone who wanted it work very hard for it.

Run 2

From the start of the second run, I knew this was going to hurt.

I could feel the lower legs threatening to cramp, and that made the whole thing more tactical than heroic. The effort was hard, but it also had to stay just controlled enough that the legs did not fully lock up.

At the same time, I was laser-focused on course awareness.

On the first run I had not really registered the turnaround properly, so this time I was actively hunting for it. I kept looking for the sign, checking the distance on the watch, and trying not to be the idiot who loses a race because he simply does not know where he is supposed to go.

That turned out to be the right instinct.

The turnaround came after a blind 90-degree turn and then a short straight of roughly 200 m, after which you turned around and came back through the same corner. It was not complicated once you knew it was there, but it was exactly the kind of detail you could miss if you were racing low on energy and oxygen.

Coming back off that left-hand turn, I finally saw Michael again. He was moving well. I did the rough arithmetic in my head and figured I had about 40 seconds with something like 1K to go. At my current pace, somewhere between 4:10 and 4:30/km, that felt like enough if I stayed composed.

One of the under-19 athletes I had passed on the bike also came back by me on the run, but that was irrelevant to my category race, so I let him go.

The important thing was that I kept moving, kept the pace from falling apart, and got to the line still ahead of Michael.

It was not a particularly fast run by any stretch of the imagination, but at least it looks like I managed some sort of negative split. Probably the first one of my running career…

Results

I crossed the line 3rd overall and 2nd in Men40-49, which made it four podiums from four races this season.

That alone would have been a satisfying way to close the year. But the finish also came with a strange twist.

After I finished, I expected Michael Shay to appear not long afterwards. He had looked close enough on the run that I assumed the final gap would be well under a minute.

Instead, he never showed up.

Eventually the results made it clear what had happened: he finished almost eight minutes behind me. There is no realistic way that gap comes from normal racing over the final 2.5K. The only explanation that makes sense is that he missed the turnaround and ran significantly farther than he should have.

That is rough. It obviously hurt his race, and selfishly it also makes my own result a bit harder to read because I would have liked a clean head-to-head finish. Still, that is part of racing too. You have to be fit enough to race hard, but you also have to pay attention to where the course actually goes.

Looking at the full results afterwards, the race was not especially deep at the front, but a podium is a podium. Only two athletes in the category broke 1:10, and I ended up just under that mark in 1:08:29.

So I will take it.

It was not a perfect race. The first run was too aggressive, the legs were never far from cramping, and the course setup had some flaws. But it was also a proper race effort, a solid result, and a much better way to end the season than the uncertainty that hung over Barrie.

Duathlon series standings

This race mattered a bit beyond the single-day result because it also changed the Men40-49 standings in the MultiSport Canada duathlon series.

Coming out of Wasaga, I moved to the top of the category with 265 points from 3 races. That puts me 10 points ahead of Robert Cardie and 30 ahead of Michael Shay.

Because I am not doing the final race, my total is locked at 265. That means the only question is how many other athletes can still get past that number with one more score.

The realistic danger comes from three people:

  • Robert Cardie needs 3rd or better.
  • Romuald Le Calonnec needs 6th or better.
  • Andrew Roberts needs 1st or 2nd; 2nd would tie me on points, but he holds the Barrie tiebreak.

Michael Shay can still pass me too, but only by winning, which looks less likely on sprint form.

Looking at the previous sprint results, the only really plausible way I fall off the podium is if Romuald wins, Andrew finishes 2nd, and Robert finishes 3rd. That is the cleanest scenario consistent with the form each of them has shown this season.

Interestingly, Michael Shay probably helps me more than he hurts me if he shows up. Unless he wins, he does not pass me in the series, but he could still take that crucial 3rd place away from Robert. If that happens, Robert stays behind me in the standings, and I likely hold on to 3rd.

Venue review

I still do not think this is a venue I would choose just for its own sake.

Removing the swim made it much more appealing than the triathlon version, but there were still some course-design details that felt rough around the edges. The main one was on the bike, where athletes entering and exiting transition were sharing the same lane late in the leg. In practice that created something close to a mandatory no-pass zone with mixed speeds and mixed handling ability, which felt messy and unnecessarily risky.

Michael was also not the only athlete to miss the turnaround on the final run. Looking through the results afterwards, I found about a dozen athletes whose second run was inconsistently longer than half of their first run, which makes it very likely that the same thing happened to them as well.

It is true that the rules clearly state that athletes are responsible for knowing the course. That part is not really debatable. Still, I think MultiSport Canada should consider stationing a volunteer at that turnaround point. It feels like a simple fix that would avoid the issue altogether.

So my view is more positive than it was two years ago, but only to a point. As a duathlon, it works well enough. As a polished race venue, I still think there is room for improvement.
