Guelph 1 Sprint Duathlon: A Duathlon, a Birthday Party, and a Shot at Edmonton

First duathlon in nine years. A surprise podium. And maybe a ticket to Edmonton 2027.

Back on the Road to Worlds

When I raced at the World Championships in Wollongong last year, Edmonton 2027 felt a long way off.

Now it suddenly feels very real.

This race was my first opportunity to earn a qualification spot for the 2027 World Multisport Championships in Edmonton, and that gave it a significance well beyond the result itself.

After making the trip halfway around the world to Australia, the prospect of racing a World Championship on home soil is incredibly appealing. No trans-Pacific flights, no major time-zone adjustments, and a much easier opportunity to share the experience with family and friends.

Of course, before thinking too much about Edmonton, there is a small challenge to deal with: I hadn’t raced a duathlon in nine years.

My previous duathlon was a 5 km run / 20 km bike / 2.5 km run format. This one flipped things around with a 2 km run / 20 km bike / 5 km run.

Still, after nine years away from the discipline, a World Championship qualifier seemed like as good a reason as any to come back.

The Qualification Game

One of the funny things about qualification races is that sometimes you finish without really knowing how well you’ve done.

Awards are presented in 10-year age groups, but qualification rankings are calculated using 5-year age groups.

That means someone standing next to you on the podium might not actually be your competition for a qualification spot. Someone who finished ahead of you could age out of your category next year. Your direct competitor might now come from the younger award group.

As if that wasn’t confusing enough, duathlon introduced another challenge for me.

Coming from years of short-course triathlon, it felt strange to race in socks. Normally, in a sprint triathlon, we do not use socks

During the race, I discovered that socks create an unexpected qualification problem.

Organizers mark athletes’ ages on their calves. In theory, that lets you know whether the person ahead of you is someone you’re actually racing for a qualification spot.

In practice, many athletes wear tall socks.

Which means the age markings disappear entirely.

So throughout the race I found myself staring at calves, trying to figure out whether the person ahead was 39, 44, or 49 years old, only to be defeated by a pair of compression socks.

And then there is the biggest unknown of all:

Who actually submitted an Expression of Interest?

A Duathlon, a Birthday Party, and a Shot at Edmonton

Elite athletes talk about training camps, altitude blocks, and carefully controlled pre-race routines.

My race preparation looked slightly different.

The day before the race was my youngest daughter’s fourth birthday party.

The schedule included decorating, entertaining kids, cleaning up afterward, and trying to convince myself that “just one more candy” was a perfectly reasonable nutritional choice before a world championship qualifier.

To be fair, I thought I had done a pretty good job. I avoided most of the obvious junk food and generally ate well throughout the day.

The only problem was that “most” and “all” are not the same thing.

Somewhere between the cake and the candy table my self-control started to fade. By race morning my stomach was politely informing me that perhaps this had not been the optimal fueling strategy.

Fortunately, things settled down before the start and never became an issue during the race itself.

Still, it felt like a fitting reminder of what age-group racing actually looks like. One day you’re hosting a birthday party. The next day you’re standing on a start line trying to qualify for a world championship.

Race Morning

As usual, I arrived ridiculously early.

About two hours early, to be precise.

That gave me plenty of time to set up transition, overthink every equipment decision, and chat with other athletes.

The morning also turned into a mini reunion.

I ran into several people I’d met at Wollongong, including one of my relay teammates. I also bumped into another athlete named Daniel. We knew we’d met somewhere before, but neither of us could remember where.

The mystery wasn’t solved until the award ceremony.

Turns out we had both raced in Wollongong.

Not Exactly My Kind of Course

The course was hilly.

Very hilly.

The temperature hovered around 24°C, which doesn’t sound extreme on paper, but it felt noticeably warm out on course.

So naturally, I approached the race with a carefully thought-out pacing strategy.

Which lasted approximately zero seconds.

Run 1: Classic Adrian

The opening 2 km started exactly as expected:

Too fast.

I quickly realized where things were heading and backed off before completely detonating my legs.

Maybe I backed off a little too much.

The result was a 9:20 split for 2 km.

Yes, it was hilly.

But it was also only 2 km.

Bike: Conservative… Maybe Too Conservative?

Transition 1 didn’t go entirely to plan.

I completely missed getting into my left cycling shoe on the first attempt and spent what felt like an eternity wrestling with it while trying to get moving.

In the process I also managed to confuse my watch and start the bike segment late.

Not ideal.

Once settled, the plan was simple: stay controlled and respect the hills.

I think I succeeded.

The question is whether I succeeded a little too well.

Looking back at the data, I suspect there may have been a bit more speed available.

Run 2: The Long Five Kilometres

Then came the final 5 km.

Hard.

Slow.

Painful.

The three words that describe most second runs in multisport racing.

My final split was 22:29, which is actually very much in line with my recent multisport performances.

Still, it felt slower than it should have.

The hills certainly didn’t help. Neither did the heat.

And perhaps my legs were more cooked than I realized from the first run and the bike.

Or maybe those earlier segments weren’t quite as conservative as they felt at the time.

A Pleasant Surprise at the Finish

When I crossed the line, I figured I might have sneaked into the top four in my category.

Maybe.

Instead, the results showed:

  • 9th Overall
  • 2nd Age Group

That was a surprise.

The winner of my category finished about 2:20 ahead of me, while I finished roughly 40 seconds ahead of third place.

And third place turned out to be Daniel.

The same Daniel who had been trying to remember where we knew each other from all morning.

So… Did I Qualify?

The short answer: I still have no idea.

At the 2027 World Championships I would race in the 40–44 age group since I’ll be 44 at the end of next year.

The challenge is that I don’t know the ages of some of the athletes around me.

One athlete from the younger category posted a faster time than I did. If he moves into the 40–44 category next year, he’ll jump ahead of me in the qualification ranking.

On the other hand, the athlete who beat me on race day may age out into the next category, which would move me up.

And, of course, nobody really knows who has submitted an Expression of Interest until the federation publishes the qualification lists.

So a second-place finish is encouraging, but it’s far from a guarantee.

For now, Edmonton remains a strong possibility rather than a certainty.

Tech Failures

No race report would be complete without a list of self-inflicted technology problems.

This week’s scorecard:

✅ Forgot the Stryd pod

✅ Forgot the Wahoo heart rate strap

✅ Managed to confuse the watch during transition

🤔 Maybe it’s finally time to trust auto-transition mode?

Final Thoughts

A second-place age-group finish in my first duathlon in nine years is difficult to complain about.

Was it perfect? No.

Could I have raced parts of it more aggressively? Probably.

But considering the long gap since my last duathlon, the challenging course, and the qualification stakes, I’ll happily take this result.

Now it’s time to recover, analyze the data, and focus on the next opportunity.

Because the next qualifier is only one week away: Rose City Welland — June 28, 2026.
